Meshing the Level Set

The next sim is a graphing calculator for surfaces defined implicitly: the user types f(x, y, z) = c and the engine shows the 2-manifold that equation carves out of ℝ³. This document compares the known ways to turn that equation into geometry, backed by the original papers, weighs where CUDA fits, and answers the standing question: can we parametrize the resulting surface?

prepared 2026-07-13 · for the level-set sim (working name) · builds on Expression, Mesh, Shader

§1 · Requirements

What this sim actually needs

Four requirements drive every judgment below. They come from what you said you want to do with the surface, not just how it should look:

RequirementConsequence
Live function input — user retypes f, surface updates Extraction must be fast enough to re-run per edit (ideally per frame, so sweeping c animates). The Expression parser already emits GLSL; the same printer can emit CUDA C.
Real vertices — arrows out of the surface, trajectories on it, flows The backbone must produce an actual engine::Mesh: positions + normals you can seed glyphs and integrate ODEs from. Image-space tricks don't qualify.
Topology honesty — as c slides, components merge, split, appear The method must not assume it knows the surface's shape in advance. This is also, as §5 shows, exactly what kills global parametrization.
CUDA on the horizon — the engine's stated end goal is GL + CUDA interop Prefer methods that are embarrassingly parallel over a grid. One family below is the canonical CUDA workload.

A quick word on the mathematical object itself. If c is a regular value of f (meaning ∇f ≠ 0 everywhere on the preimage), the preimage theorem guarantees f⁻¹(c) is a smooth, orientable 2-manifold embedded in ℝ³ — a genuinely nice surface. And by Sard's theorem, almost every c (all but a measure-zero set) is regular. The bad values are the interesting ones: they are the critical values of f, and crossing one changes the surface's topology (Morse theory — see §5). The sim will hit them whenever the user slides c; the pipeline just has to survive the frame, not be clever about it.

§2 · The elephant in the shader

Why ray marching is a mode, not the backbone

Your instinct is correct. Ray marching (walking each camera ray through the volume in the fragment shader until f − c changes sign) renders implicit surfaces beautifully and would reuse the domain-coloring codegen almost verbatim — but its output is pixels. There is no vertex anywhere. Nothing to attach an arrow to, nothing to integrate a trajectory on, nothing for CUDA to compute over. Everything lives and dies inside one fragment shader invocation.

That said, don't discard it — keep it in the back pocket as a reference renderer: it shows the exact surface with no discretization, so it's the ground truth you compare your mesh against (is marching cubes missing a thin tube? toggle the ray-marched view and see). It's also the cheapest possible first picture while a mesh is still being extracted. The literature here is solid: Hart's sphere tracing for Lipschitz-bounded fields, Mitchell's interval-arithmetic root isolation for robustness with arbitrary f, and — most relevant to this engine — Keeter's 2020 paper on evaluating compiled closed-form expressions massively in parallel on the GPU, which is philosophically the same trick Expression already plays.

  • Hart, “Sphere Tracing: A Geometric Method for the Antialiased Ray Tracing of Implicit Surfaces,” The Visual Computer 12, 1996. doi:10.1007/s003710050084
  • Mitchell, “Robust Ray Intersection with Interval Arithmetic,” Graphics Interface, 1990.
  • Keeter, “Massively Parallel Rendering of Complex Closed-Form Implicit Surfaces,” ACM SIGGRAPH, 2020. doi:10.1145/3386569.3392429
§3 · The candidates

Mesh extraction methods, compared

All serious contenders share one skeleton: sample f on some spatial structure, find where the sign of f − c flips, build triangles there. They differ in what structure they sample, where they place vertices, and what they can promise about the result. Ordered by implementation effort — the order doubles as a plausible build order.

M1

Marching Cubes (and the simplex fix, Marching Tetrahedra)

recommended backbone

Sample f on a uniform N³ grid. Each cube of 8 samples gets an 8-bit code from the signs of f − c at its corners; a 256-entry lookup table says which triangles the surface cuts through that cube, and vertex positions come from linear interpolation along the crossed edges. That's the whole algorithm — the 1987 Lorensen–Cline paper is one of the most cited papers in all of computer graphics because the idea is exactly this simple.

The classic table has ambiguous cases that can leave pinhole cracks; two standard fixes exist. Chernyaev's MC33 extends the table to resolve every case consistently, and the asymptotic decider disambiguates faces analytically. Alternatively, Marching Tetrahedra splits each cube into 5–6 tetrahedra — a simplex has no ambiguous configurations at all, at the price of more, skinnier triangles.

Fits engine
Output is exactly an indexed triangle list → engine::Mesh with layout {3, 3} (position + normal). Normals come free and exactly from the analytic gradient ∇f — see §4.
Weakness
Vertices sit on grid edges, so quality depends on resolution; sharp creases get rounded (rarely an issue for smooth level sets of analytic f).
Why first
Simple enough to have running on CPU in a day, and it is the single most parallelized meshing algorithm in existence — the CUDA path in §4 is this method.

Papers

  • Lorensen, Cline, “Marching Cubes: A High Resolution 3D Surface Construction Algorithm,” ACM SIGGRAPH, 1987. doi:10.1145/37401.37422
  • Chernyaev, “Marching Cubes 33: Construction of Topologically Correct Isosurfaces,” CERN technical report CN/95-17, 1995.
  • Nielson, Hamann, “The Asymptotic Decider: Resolving the Ambiguity in Marching Cubes,” IEEE Visualization, 1991.

M2

Dual methods — Surface Nets & Dual Contouring

upgrade path

Same grid, opposite philosophy: instead of putting several vertices on the edges of each crossed cell, put one vertex inside each crossed cell and connect neighbors into quads. Surface Nets places that vertex by relaxation; Dual Contouring places it by minimizing a quadratic error function built from Hermite data — surface points plus normals on the crossed edges. The payoff is much better vertex placement, natural quad meshes (nicer for flows and any future subdivision), and faithful sharp features.

The key input, exact normals on edge crossings, is cheap for this engine: the normal of a level set is just ∇f / |∇f|, and Expression can be taught to emit ∇f symbolically (§4). That makes Dual Contouring unusually attractive here compared to engines that only have sampled data.

Fits engine
Drop-in replacement for M1 at the same grid; produces quads → triangulate into Mesh. Shares all CUDA infrastructure with M1.
Weakness
Plain DC can produce non-manifold edges in nasty configurations; the “manifold dual contouring” follow-up repairs this. More bookkeeping than M1.
When
After M1 works and you find yourself wanting better-shaped triangles for the flow integrator.

Papers

  • Gibson, “Constrained Elastic Surface Nets: Generating Smooth Surfaces from Binary Segmented Data,” MICCAI, 1998.
  • Ju, Losasso, Schaefer, Warren, “Dual Contouring of Hermite Data,” ACM SIGGRAPH, 2002. doi:10.1145/566654.566586
  • Schaefer, Warren, “Dual Marching Cubes: Primal Contouring of Dual Grids,” Pacific Graphics, 2004.
  • Schaefer, Ju, Warren, “Manifold Dual Contouring,” IEEE TVCG 13(3), 2007.
M3

Continuation / surface tracking (“marching triangles”)

elegant but fragile

Forget the grid: start from one seed point on the surface, and grow a triangle fan outward along it — each new vertex is predicted in the tangent plane and Newton-projected back onto f = c. Bloomenthal's continuation and Hartmann's marching method produce gorgeous, nearly-equilateral, curvature-adapted meshes, and they only ever evaluate f near the surface instead of over a whole volume.

The fatal flaw for this sim: it finds only the connected component containing the seed. A user's f = c routinely has five components the sim doesn't know about, and as c slides across a critical value new components are born with no seed to grow from. You'd need a grid search to find seeds anyway — at which point M1 already did the job. Worth reading for the predict–project step, which is exactly the machinery the flow integrator reuses (§5).

Papers

  • Bloomenthal, “Polygonization of Implicit Surfaces,” Computer Aided Geometric Design 5, 1988.
  • Hilton, Stoddart, Illingworth, Windeatt, “Marching Triangles: Range Image Fusion for Complex Object Modelling,” ICIP, 1996.
  • Hartmann, “A Marching Method for the Triangulation of Surfaces,” The Visual Computer 14, 1998.
M4

Particle systems on the surface

not a mesher — the glyph engine

Witkin & Heckbert's classic: scatter particles, constrain them to f = c with the same Newton projection as M3, and let them repel each other until they blanket the surface uniformly. The output isn't a mesh — it's a perfectly even sampling of the manifold, each sample carrying its position and normal.

Read that against your requirements again: “stick arrows out of the manifold, simulate trajectories.” Particles are the arrow anchors and the trajectory seeds. This method doesn't compete with M1 — it composes with it: the mesh (M1/M2) is what you see, the particles are what the vector-field layer computes on. Each particle is one CUDA thread doing a few evaluations of f and ∇f per step — the cleanest possible first CUDA kernel for this engine.

Papers

  • Witkin, Heckbert, “Using Particles to Sample and Control Implicit Surfaces,” ACM SIGGRAPH, 1994. doi:10.1145/192161.192227
  • Meyer, Kirby, Whitaker, “Topology, Accuracy, and Quality of Isosurface Meshes Using Dynamic Particles,” IEEE TVCG 13(6), 2007.
M5

Guaranteed-topology meshing

research-grade · later, if ever

Everything above can, at finite resolution, miss a thin handle and silently report the wrong genus. If the sim ever wants to make topological claims (“this surface has genus 3”), there is a literature that certifies the mesh is isotopic to the true level set: Plantinga–Vegter drive an octree subdivision with interval arithmetic until a topological correctness criterion holds; Boissonnat–Oudot do provably-good Delaunay sampling. Costs: interval evaluation of f (Expression would need an interval evaluator — doable, it's the same tree walk), and running time that spikes near critical values. File under “when the sim grows a topology readout.”

Papers

  • Plantinga, Vegter, “Isotopic Approximation of Implicit Curves and Surfaces,” Symposium on Geometry Processing, 2004.
  • Boissonnat, Oudot, “Provably Good Sampling and Meshing of Surfaces,” Graphical Models 67, 2005.
  • Cheng, Dey, Shewchuk, Delaunay Mesh Generation, CRC Press, 2012. (the book on this)

§4 · Compute

Where CUDA comes in — and yes, it's time

Marching cubes is not just compatible with CUDA; it is the algorithm NVIDIA ships as the flagship interop sample, and the reason is structural: every stage is data-parallel with no communication between neighbors.

grid eval one thread per grid point: F[i,j,k] = f(x,y,z) − c cell classify one thread per cell: 8 sign bits → triangle count (table) compaction exclusive prefix sum (CUB/Thrust) → where each cell writes vertex gen one thread per output triangle → writes straight into the VBO

The last line is the important one for this engine. CUDA can register an OpenGL buffer object (cudaGraphicsGLRegisterBuffer), map it, and have kernels write vertex data directly into the memory OpenGL will draw from — zero copies, nothing ever touches the CPU. That is precisely the “Mesh is the future CUDA-interop point” design decision already recorded in the engine's architecture notes, now with its first real customer. Re-extracting a 128³ grid this way runs at real-time rates on any modern GPU, which is what makes animating the sweep of c — watching handles form and split live — feasible.

Two engine-specific notes, both cheap and both multipliers:

Codegen, again. Expression already walks its tree to print GLSL. Printing CUDA C from the same tree is the same walk with different spellings (NVRTC can compile it at runtime, exactly like Shader::fromSources does for GLSL). One parser, three backends: CPU eval, GLSL, CUDA.

Symbolic gradients. Differentiating the expression tree is a mechanical transform (each node type has a derivative rule — automatic differentiation of the AST). That one feature feeds three consumers: exact mesh normals for M1/M2 (n = ∇f/|∇f|, strictly better than finite differences), Hermite data for M2, and the tangent projector for §5. It's the highest-leverage small feature in this whole document.

§5 · The math question

Is a parametrization even possible?

Short version: locally, always. Globally with one chart, provably never (for the closed surfaces this sim will mostly produce). And for flows you don't need one at all. The long version, as a chain of questions:

Can I parametrize a piece of the surface?

Yes, unconditionally, wherever ∇f ≠ 0. That is the implicit function theorem verbatim: if, say, ∂f/∂z ≠ 0 at a point, then near that point the surface is exactly a graph z = g(x, y) — a smooth chart with parametrization (u, v) ↦ (u, v, g(u,v)). The surface is covered by such charts; that is literally what makes it a manifold. Every local operation you care about (tangent planes, geodesic steps, texture decals) is available through these.

Can one chart cover the whole surface?

No, whenever the level set is compact (a sphere, a torus, a tangle — the typical case for polynomial f with bounded components). A single parametrization from a planar domain would make the surface homeomorphic to an open subset of ℝ², and a compact surface never is (it would have to be both compact and open in ℝ², hence empty). The sphere already defeats it — that's why every world map either cuts or distorts. This isn't a limitation of technique; it's topology.

What's the best a global structure can do, then?

An atlas of overlapping charts (which the mesh effectively is), or a parametrization over a canonical domain with controlled seams/singular points. The uniformization theorem classifies what's canonically possible: genus 0 surfaces map conformally to the sphere, genus 1 to a flat torus, genus ≥ 2 to hyperbolic quotients. There is a mature computational literature that takes a triangle mesh and produces such structures — conformal parametrization, spherical parametrization, global seamless quad layouts. It's genuinely beautiful work, and all of it starts from a mesh, which is another argument for §3 producing one.

But note the fine print for this sim: any such global structure is computed per surface. The moment the user drags c across a critical value of f, the genus changes (Morse theory: crossing a critical point attaches a handle or splits a component), and the previous parametrization isn't just stale — its domain is the wrong topological type. A “parametrize once, reuse forever” plan cannot survive a c-slider. Parametrization, if ever built, is a per-frame derived artifact, not a foundation.

So how do flows work without one?

Extrinsically — work in ℝ³ and never leave the surface, using only f and ∇f. Two operations do everything:

tangent projection P(x) = I − n nᵀ, n = ∇f(x)/|∇f(x)| (any ambient vector field V becomes a surface field P·V) Newton retraction x ← x − f(x)·∇f(x)/|∇f(x)|² (pulls a drifted point back onto f = c, quadratically)

A trajectory is then: step the ODE with the projected field (RK4), retract, repeat. An arrow is: particle position + any field evaluated there, projected or not (surface normals themselves are the simplest “arrows sticking out of the manifold” — they're just ∇f). This is the same predict–project machinery as M3/M4, one more reason those papers are on the list. For full PDEs on the surface (heat flow, reaction–diffusion patterns, advection of dye), the closest point method solves them on an ambient grid around the surface with standard 3D stencils — no parametrization, no mesh operators — which composes perfectly with a CUDA grid pipeline that already exists for M1.

Verdict on parametrization

Local charts: guaranteed by the implicit function theorem wherever ∇f ≠ 0. One global chart: topologically impossible for closed surfaces. Global atlases/quad layouts: possible post-meshing, expensive, and invalidated every time c crosses a critical value. Design the sim so nothing needs a parametrization: mesh for display, particles + projection/retraction for flows, closest-point for PDEs. The gradient ∇f is the whole API.

§6 · Reading order

Suggested reading sequence

Ordered so each paper sets up the next; the first three are enough to start building.

  1. Lorensen & Cline 1987 — the backbone algorithm; short and readable.
  2. do Carmo §2-2/2-3 — regular surfaces & the implicit function theorem; makes §5 rigorous in ~20 pages.
  3. NVIDIA marchingCubes sample + Dyken 2008 — the CUDA formulation and the interop pattern.
  4. Witkin & Heckbert 1994 — particles, projection, and constraint maintenance; the flow layer's toolbox.
  5. Ju et al. 2002 — dual contouring, for when mesh quality starts to matter.
  6. Milnor ch. 1 — why the surface changes topology under the c-slider, and why that's a feature.
  7. Ruuth & Merz 2008 — PDEs on the surface with the grid you already have.
  8. Floater & Hormann 2005 — read last, to confirm parametrization is a luxury, not a dependency.
